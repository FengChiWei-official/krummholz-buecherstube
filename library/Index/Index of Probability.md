## Definition

Probability theory as a discipline — the developing map of its concepts. Read it not as a static catalog but as the successive answers to successive problems: each era confronted a limit in the concepts it had, invented a new one, and thereby opened the next question. The six canonical notes are the stations where this development settled.

## The Conceptual Movement

### 1. Games of Chance: Expectation (1654–1713)

**Problem.** How to divide the stakes of an unfinished game fairly (Pascal–Fermat's problem of points).

**Concept.** Probability as counting equally-likely outcomes; Huygens' expectation — the value of a lottery.

**Limit.** This works only for finite, symmetric spaces. No construction yet reaches the world's asymmetric, empirical uncertainty.

### 2. From Games to the World: the Law of Large Numbers (1713)

**Problem.** Does individual randomness produce aggregate regularity?

**Concept.** Bernoulli's law of large numbers: in repeated trials the observed frequency converges to the underlying probability. Probability gains an empirical meaning — a bridge from games to the world.

**Limit.** LLN asserts convergence, but not what shape the aggregate takes or how fast it settles.

### 3. The Shape of Aggregates: the Normal Curve (1733–1812)

**Problem.** What form does a large aggregate of independent contributions take?

**Concept.** De Moivre's normal approximation, Laplace's central limit theorem, Gauss's law of errors. The aggregate acquires its own stable shape — the Normal — independent of the individual law. This is the first universal theorem.

**Limit.** Justified case by case; nothing yet explains why the same shape recurs, and rare events behave differently.

### 4. Reasoning Backward: Inverse Probability (1763–1815)

**Problem.** Given data, what is the probability of the hypothesis that produced it?

**Concept.** Bayes and Laplace invert the direction: from effect to cause — conditional probability as inference.

**Limit.** This opens the dispute over what probability is (frequency vs. degree of belief), a crisis unresolved until the measure-theoretic settlement.

### 5. The Law of Small Numbers: Poisson (1837)

**Problem.** Not large aggregates but rare events: counts that are usually zero.

**Concept.** Poisson's law of small numbers — Binomial(n, p) with n → ∞, p → 0, np = λ converges to Poisson, a second universal attractor for counts.

**Limit.** Poisson appears as both a count and a waiting time; the two faces are not yet unified into one process.

### 6. The Foundational Crisis and the Measure-Theoretic Settlement (1906–1933)

**Problem.** What is probability — frequency or belief? Every founding attempt is intuitive and unaxiomatic.

**Concept.** Markov adds dependence (chains); Borel imports measure theory; Kolmogorov settles it: probability is a measure, and a random variable a measurable function. Frequency and belief are reduced to instances of one object. The pushforward view — a distribution is the image of the measure under the map — is the mature form.

**Limit.** The measure settles what probability is, but the objects of study are now static; time is not yet in the picture.

### 7. Processes in Time: the Modern Synthesis (1920s–1950s)

**Concept.** Markov chains, Wiener's Brownian motion, Lévy processes, Doob's martingales: probability becomes the study of processes evolving in time. From this and the limit theorems grows the modern two-layer view — concrete processes vs. universal limit theorems, with Normal and Poisson as 'homeless' attractors — and the scenes from which every standard distribution arises.
